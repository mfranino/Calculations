Engineering assumptions

Uniform elastic uniaxial shaft stress, isotropic material, correctly wired four-active-gauge full bridge with axial and transverse gauges at diametrically opposite locations. Ideal bridge response: |Vout/Vexc| ≈ GF(1+ν)|ε|/2. The sign depends on bridge wiring.

Shunt calibration verifies electrical response, not mechanical force sensitivity. For turbine use, verify mechanical calibration, bending/torsion rejection, temperature compensation, telemetry bandwidth and time synchronization. Axial load at the instrumented shaft section is not necessarily identical to hydraulic runner thrust.

Measurement of axial force using strain gauges

Axial force can be measured using strain gauges bonded to a structural component that carries the axial load. The gauges measure the elastic deformation of the material, which can then be converted into force.

For hydropower applications, this method can be used to measure axial thrust transmitted through a turbine shaft, thrust-bearing support, or another load-carrying component.

1. Measurement principle

For a component subjected to pure axial force:

σ = FA
(8)

Using Hooke's law:

ε = σE
(9)

Therefore:

F = E A ε
(10)

Where:

  • F — axial force [N]
  • E — Young's modulus [Pa]
  • A — effective cross-sectional area [m²]
  • ε — measured axial strain [m/m]

For steel, Young's modulus is typically around 200–210 GPa.

The main challenge is that strain gauges measure local strain, not force directly. The relationship between measured strain and axial force depends on the component geometry, load distribution, bending, temperature, and mounting position.

Theory and measurement principles

The measurement is based on linear elastic strain in a shaft subjected to an axial force. The following equations use the ideal uniaxial-stress approximation and a four-active-gauge full Wheatstone bridge. Equation numbers follow scientific-paper conventions.

Governing equations

The nominal axial stress is given by

σz = FzA
(1)

For a hollow circular shaft, the effective cross-sectional area is

A = π4 (Do2 − Di2)
(2)

Hooke's law relates axial strain to axial stress:

εz = σzE = FzEA
(3)

For an ideal uniaxial stress state, transverse strain follows from Poisson's ratio:

εt = −ν εz
(4)

For a matched four-active-gauge bridge with two axial and two transverse gauges, the first-order normalized bridge output magnitude is

|VoutVex| ≈ GF2 (1 + ν) |εz|
(5)

Consequently, the axial-force magnitude estimated from bridge output is

|Fz| ≈ 2EAGF(1 + ν) |VoutVex|
(6)

The time-dependent force can be separated into mean and fluctuating components:

Fz(t) = F̄z + F′z(t)
(7)

5. Static and dynamic force

Write F(t) = F̄ + F′(t). Determine the static component using a defined averaging interval or low-pass filter, and analyze the dynamic component using time traces, FFT, RMS, peak-to-peak and rotational orders. For a nominal 125 rpm shaft, 1× = 2.083 Hz, 9× = 18.75 Hz and 24× = 50 Hz. These are reference frequencies, not necessarily measured force peaks.

6. Calibration and uncertainty

  1. Inspect the gauge bonds and measure bridge and insulation resistance.
  2. Check excitation, bridge polarity, zero output and shunt calibration. Shunt calibration checks the electrical chain only.
  3. Where practical, apply known axial loads over the intended range in increasing and decreasing sequences; fit force against bridge output.
  4. Measure zero drift and sensitivity versus shaft temperature.
  5. Verify dynamic gain, phase, sampling, anti-aliasing, telemetry latency and packet loss over the required frequency range.
  6. Assess residual sensitivity to bending, torsion, gauge alignment, elastic modulus, geometry, and calibration uncertainty.

Typical preliminary DAQ targets: DC–500 Hz bandwidth, ≥5 kS/s sampling (10 kS/s preferred), bridge excitation around 2.5–5 V subject to gauge specifications, and simultaneous temperature and speed acquisition. Final values must be selected from the actual force spectrum and accuracy requirements.
